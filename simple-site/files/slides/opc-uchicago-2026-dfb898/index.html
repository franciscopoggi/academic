Off-Protocol Communication
Francisco Poggi
University of Mannheim
University of Chicago · 2026

Motivation

  • Communication is sometimes organized around a protocol: who speaks, when, what messages are allowed.
    • E.g., peer review, courtroom procedure, expert committees, internal reporting.
  • Protocols shape information transmission and hence outcomes.
  • However, participants may also communicate off protocol:
    • referee and editor at a conference dinner;
    • ex parte communication with the judge;
    • employee and supervisor in the hallway.
Questions
When does off-protocol communication undermine the protocol?
How can protocols be designed to be robust to off-protocol communication?
Examples
Two Simple Examples
To fix ideas about the kind of deviations we have in mind.

Example I: Assigning an Unpleasant Task

  • $n$ workers $i=1,\dots,n$.
  • A supervisor assigns a task to one of them, or to nobody.
  • Private cost $c_i\in\{\mathcal{l},\mathcal{h}\}$, independent, $p:=\Pr(c_i=\mathcal{h})$.
  • Payoffs. Each worker gets $1$ if the task is performed; the assignee $i$ also pays $c_i$.
    • Supervisor: $1-c_i$ if performed, $0$ otherwise.
  • $\mathcal{l}<1<\mathcal{h}$: a low-cost worker is worth assigning; a high-cost one is not.
First best
Assign the task to a low-cost worker if there is one; otherwise, assign it to nobody.

Example I: Incentives and a Protocol

  • $n=1$: supervisor and worker have the same payoff. Nothing to design: ask him.
  • $n\ge 2$: free-riding. A low-cost worker prefers that another worker do it.
A protocol
Workers simultaneously report their costs.
Assign the task uniformly at random among the workers who report a low cost.
If nobody reports a low cost, assign it to nobody.
  • Obedience. If workers report truthfully, the supervisor is happy to follow through with the protocol.
  • Truthful reporting is an equilibrium if $p$ is high enough: $$\mathcal{l}\ \le\ \frac{n\,(1-p)\,p^{\,n-1}}{1-p^{\,n}}$$
With $n=1$, strictly speaking neologism-proofness selects the most informative equilibria of this cheap-talk game.

Condition: a low-cost worker who reports $L$ gets $1-\mathcal{l}\,\E[1/(k+1)]$, where $k\sim\mathrm{Bin}(n-1,1-p)$ is the number of other low reports; $\E[1/(k+1)]=\frac{1-p^n}{n(1-p)}$. Reporting $H$ gives $\Pr(k\ge1)=1-p^{n-1}$. Truthful iff $\mathcal{l}\,\frac{1-p^n}{n(1-p)}\le p^{n-1}$. For $n=2$ this is $p\ge\mathcal{l}/(2-\mathcal{l})$. The high type's constraint holds automatically since $\mathcal{h}>1\ge\frac{n(1-p)p^{n-1}}{1-p^n}$.

A Speech

A low-cost worker reports $H$. Then he meets the supervisor in the hallway and privately tells him:

"If the protocol says nobody, assign the task to me. Otherwise follow its recommendation."

What should the supervisor make of this?

  • The worker gains. He free-rides whenever a colleague is low, and the task still gets done when nobody else is.
  • A high-cost worker would never say this. It would get him the task exactly when nobody else is low: $1-\mathcal{h}<0$.
  • So the supervisor believes him, and is happy to do as asked: when the protocol says nobody, this worker is the only low-cost one.

The protocol unravels. Reporting truthfully is no longer optimal once the worker can talk to the supervisor off protocol.

Example II: Experts with Career Concerns

  • A decision-maker must take an action.
  • Consults two advisers: one is an expert, the other is a quack.
  • The decision-maker cannot distinguish them.
  • The expert knows the best course of action; the quack does not.
  • Both want the correct action taken, but also care about their reputation:
    • the expert wants to be recognized as the expert;
    • the quack wants to pool with the expert.
Protocol that maximizes the quality of the action
Advisers submit a joint report, with no attribution of individual recommendations.
Individual recommendations are unobserved, so career concerns do not distort truthful reporting.

But the expert can communicate off protocol:

"I'm the expert. I deliberately sabotaged the joint report. Ignore it; here is the correct action instead…"

Is this credible?

What We Do

  • Study communication protocols when senders can also talk to the receiver off protocol.
    • A designer chooses the protocol: who communicates, what messages are allowed, how they are processed.
    • She cannot prevent a sender from addressing the receiver directly (privately, cheaply).
    • No commitment: the receiver cannot promise to ignore what she hears outside the protocol.
  • Propose a robustness criterion based on the credibility of speeches.
    • A speech makes claims about the sender's type and behavior, and recommends what the receiver should do.
    • It is credible if
      • the receiver would comply when taking it at face value;
      • complying is profitable to the sender only if the speech is true.
Robustness criterion
No credible off-protocol speech.   Deliberately demanding: we see this as a minimal notion.

Preview of Results

  • Proof of concept. Speeches as a way to discipline communication mechanisms.
  • Experts with career concerns.
    • Weak career concerns: robust protocols trade decision accuracy against concealment — reveal more about expertise, or decide less well.
    • Strong career concerns: the quack's appetite for promotion protects the protocol; perfect concealment survives.
  • General results.
    • One sender: robustness is neologism-proofness (Farrell 1993). A claim about what I did inside the protocol adds nothing to a claim about who I am. Direct mechanisms are without loss.
    • Multiple senders: claims about protocol behavior have bite. Direct reports remain without loss; direct recommendations do not.

Related Literature

  • Neologism-proofness (Farrell 1993); natural language equilibrium (Reny 2025).
    • Equilibrium refinements in cheap-talk and signaling games.
    • This paper: speeches as additional deviations from a designed communication protocol.
  • Communication among mechanism participants.
    • Collusion-proof mechanism design: agents write side deals.
    • Akbarpour and Li (2020), credibility: the designer may misrepresent what happened in the mechanism.
  • Main application: Ottaviani and Sørensen (2006) — incentives to appear informed degrade communication.

Application
Expert Advice with Career Concerns

Expert Advice with Career Concerns: Model

  • Players: two senders $i=1,2$ and one receiver.
  • One sender is an expert ($E$), the other a quack ($Q$).
  • Roles are private; each assignment is equally likely.
  • Payoff-relevant state $\omega\in\{L,R\}$, uniform prior.
  • Each sender observes a conditionally independent binary signal $s_i\in\{l,r\}$ with precision $p_E$ and $p_Q$.
    • For exposition, $p_Q=1/2$: the quack is completely uninformed.
  • The receiver chooses an action $a\in\{L,R\}$ and a promotion $\pi\in\{1,2\}$.
Payoffs
Everyone receives $1$ if $a=\omega$.
Sender $i$ gets an additional bonus $B>0$ iff promoted ($\pi=i$).
The receiver gets an additional bonus $D>0$ if the expert is promoted ($\pi=E$).

Direct Revelation Mechanisms

  • A direct revelation mechanism asks each sender to report whether they are the expert, and their signal realization.
    • Given the uninformativeness of the quack's signal, we can simplify to $M_i=\{L,R,Q\}$.
  • It recommends an action and a promotion to the receiver, $(a,\pi)\in\{L,R\}\times\{1,2\}$.
$$\tau:\ \{L,R,Q\}^2 \;\to\; \Delta\big(\{L,R\}\times\{1,2\}\big)$$
Truthful and obedient
A direct revelation mechanism is truthful and obedient if there is an equilibrium in which senders report truthfully and the receiver follows the recommendation.

The Revelation Principle (Myerson 1982) justifies the focus on direct revelation mechanisms (absent speeches).

A Symmetric Family

  • We focus on a symmetric family of direct revelation mechanisms:
    • both senders are treated symmetrically;
    • both states and actions are treated symmetrically.
  • Under truthful reports, a symmetric mechanism is summarized by two numbers: $$x=\Pr(a=\omega \mid \text{truthful reports}),\qquad y=\Pr(\pi=E \mid \text{truthful reports}).$$
  • We must also define what happens after inconsistent reports.
Inconsistent reports
Both senders claim to be the quack: $(a,\pi)$ drawn uniformly at random.
Both senders claim to be the expert:
  • reports do not coincide: $(a,\pi)$ drawn uniformly at random;
  • reports coincide: the action matches the reports with probability $\alpha$.
($\alpha=1/2$: every inconsistency leads to a uniform draw.)
A similar application can be constructed with independent types. There are then no inconsistent reports, but more cases to consider.
Symmetry is without loss: the environment is invariant under permutations of sender identities and simultaneous exchanges of state, signal, and action labels.

Truthfulness and Obedience ($\alpha=1/2$)(general $\alpha$)

Obedience
The receiver follows the recommendation iff $x\ge \tfrac12$ and $y\ge \tfrac12$.
Expert truthfulness
$$\underbrace{U_E \;:=\; x + y\,B}_{\text{truthful report}} \;\ge\; \max\Big\{\underbrace{\tfrac12+\tfrac{B}{2}}_{\text{claim to be the quack}},\;\; \underbrace{(1-x)+y\,B}_{\text{flip the signal}}\Big\}$$ Implied by obedience. Claiming $Q$ makes the reports inconsistent (uniform draw).
Quack truthfulness
$$\underbrace{U_Q \;:=\; x + (1-y)\,B}_{\text{truthful report}} \;\ge\; \underbrace{\tfrac12+\tfrac{B}{2}}_{\text{claim to be the expert}}$$
$$\underbrace{U_Q \;:=\; x + (1-y)\,B}_{\text{truthful report}} \;\ge\; \underbrace{\colorbox{#f3d9da}{$\textcolor{#b5121b}{\tilde{x}(\alpha)}$}+\tfrac{B}{2}}_{\text{claim to be the expert}}$$
Not implied by obedience. Claiming to be the expert also makes the reports inconsistent (uniform draw). $\tilde{x}(\alpha)$: accuracy of the recommended action when the quack pretends to be the expert.
Last step switches the slide from $\alpha=1/2$ to general $\alpha$. Obedience and expert truthfulness are untouched: the expert's deviation to $Q$ yields two $Q$ claims, which is a uniform draw for every $\alpha$. Only the quack's deviation (both claim expert) is affected: $\tilde{x}(\alpha)=\tfrac12[\alpha p_E+(1-\alpha)(1-p_E)]+\tfrac14$, so $\tilde{x}(\tfrac12)=\tfrac12$ and $\tilde{x}\in[\tfrac34-\tfrac{p_E}{2},\,\tfrac14+\tfrac{p_E}{2}]$.
$\tilde{x}(\alpha)$: derivation

Symmetric Mechanisms in the Unit Square

  • By obedience, $x,y\ge\tfrac12$: an obedient, symmetric mechanism is a point $$(2x-1,\;2y-1)\in[0,1]^2.$$
    • horizontal: decision accuracy;
    • vertical: how often the expert is promoted.
  • Feasibility. The decision cannot be more accurate than the expert's signal: $x\le p_E$.
  • Quack's iso-payoff curves. $U_Q=x+(1-y)B$: parallel lines of slope $1/B$; $U_Q$ rises to the south-east.
  • Quack truthfulness. $U_Q$ must be at least its value at the origin, $\tfrac12+\tfrac B2$: the region below the iso-payoff line through the origin.
General $\alpha$

Designer Objectives

So far we have only looked at what can be implemented. Let us give the designer an objective.

  • The designer gets $1$ if $a=\omega$, plus $b$ if the expert is promoted.
  • Expected payoff under truthful reports: $\;x + b\,y$.
  • $b>0$: wants the expert promoted.   $b<0$: wants the expert not promoted.
Weak career concerns: $B < 2p_E-1$
The designer attains his maximum payoff: truthfulness does not bind.
  • Full credit if $b>0$: $\;y=1$.
  • No credit if $b<0$: $\;y=\tfrac12$.
Strong career concerns: $B > 2p_E-1$
Quack truthfulness binds when the designer wants to promote the expert.
  • Maximum credit if $b>0$: $\;y<1$.
  • No credit if $b<0$: $\;y=\tfrac12$.

In every case the decision is as accurate as it can be: $x=p_E$.

Robustness
Off-Protocol Communication

Off-Protocol Communication

Timing (informal):

  1. Senders observe their types.
  2. Senders and receiver participate in the mechanism.
  3. Senders communicate with the receiver off protocol.
  4. Receiver takes an action.
Approach
We do not want to fix any particular form of off-protocol communication. Instead, we look at speeches.

Two Types of Speeches

"Simple" speeches: about private information only
"I'm the expert. Promote me."
"I'm the expert, my signal was $s$. Play $s$ and promote me."
"Complex" speeches: about private information and behavior
"I'm the expert, and I lied in my report. Flip the recommended action; and since a quack would not benefit from asking you this, promote me."

Speeches and Credibility

A speech by a sender consists of:

  • a claim about her type: a set $S$ of types ("I am one of these");
  • for complex speeches, a claim about her report: how she played in the protocol;
  • a recommendation: what the receiver should do, as a function of what the protocol tells him.
Credibility
Receiver: taking the speech at face value, he is willing to follow the recommendation.
Sender: the types who gain from the recommendation being followed are exactly the claimed ones.
Behavior (complex speeches): the claimed report is optimal given that the recommendation is followed.
Robustness
A mechanism is robust to off-protocol communication iff there is no credible speech.
Formal definition

Simple Speech

"I'm the expert, got signal $L$. Play $L$ and promote me."
Profitable for the expert iff
$$U_E \;:=\; x + y\,B \;\le\; p_E + B$$ (strict if $(x,y)\neq(p_E,1)$)
Profitable for the quack iff
$$U_Q \;\le\; \tfrac12 + B$$
Credible iff $\;U_Q \ge \tfrac12 + B$
Robustness requires $\;U_Q \le \tfrac12 + B$.

Caveat: one must also check that the expert with signal $R$ profits from a credible speech, $U_E \le (1-p_E)+B$. But if this holds, the quack profits as well.

Complex Speech

"I'm the expert and was not truthful. Do not follow the action recommendation, and promote me."
Profitable for the expert iff
$$U_E \;\le\; x + B$$ (strict if $y<1$)
Profitable for the quack iff
$$U_Q \;\le\; \big[1-\tilde{x}(\alpha)\big] + B$$
Robustness requires
$$U_Q \;\le\; \big[1-\tilde{x}(\alpha)\big] + B$$

Quack's Complex Speech and Binding Constraints

"I'm the quack and I claimed to be the expert. Thus, flip the action and don't promote me."
  • Profitable iff $\;U_Q < 1-\tilde{x}(\alpha)$.
  • Not binding for $\tilde{x}(\alpha)=\tfrac12$.
Binding constraints for $\tilde{x}(\alpha)=\tfrac12$
Incentive compatibility and robustness amount to $$U_Q \;\in\; \Big[\tfrac12+\tfrac{B}{2}\,,\;\tfrac12+B\Big].$$

Robustness to the Simple Speech ($\alpha=1/2$)

  • Quack truthfulness: $U_Q\ \ge\ \tfrac12+\tfrac B2$.
  • Robustness: $U_Q\ \le\ \tfrac12+B$.
  • Mechanisms with accurate decisions and little credit for the expert invite "I'm the expert, promote me."

Intuition

$\tilde{x}(\alpha)$ plays a dual role:

  • punish on-protocol deviations by the quack;
  • make off-protocol deviations by the quack more attractive, rendering the expert's deviation non-credible.

Both effects push $\tilde{x}(\alpha)$ down.

Binding constraints
Obedience, and two constraints on the quack's payoff.
Two ways to lower the quack's payoff $\;U_Q = x + (1-y)\,B$
Promote him less often (higher $y$): give the quack less credit.
Decide less accurately (lower $x$): reduce the precision of the action.

Designer Payoffs under Robustness

The designer maximizes $\;x + b\,y\;$ subject to robustness. He chooses:

Weak career concerns: $B < 2p_E-1$
  • Full credit $\;(x,y)=(p_E,\,1)\;$ if $b>0$.
  • Minimum credit $\;(x,y)=(p_E,\,\underline{y})\;$ if $b\in(-B,0)$.
  • No credit and garble $\;(x,y)=(\underline{p},\,\tfrac12)\;$ if $b<-B$.

Transition Questions

  • We used direct revelation mechanisms.
    • These are without loss if we do not care about speeches.
    • Can anything implementable with a general mechanism robust to off-protocol communication also be implemented with a robust direct mechanism?
  • In the application there was no welfare loss in setting $\alpha$ small: it only affected outcomes off path.
    • A modification with two possible experts remains tractable.
    • There, reducing $\tilde{x}(\alpha)$ is costly: the designer faces a new trade-off.
  • Given low $\alpha$, the "complex speeches" were not binding.
    • In general, under what conditions is it enough to check simple speeches?
Part II
General Model

Setup

  • A receiver, $i=0$, and $n$ senders $i=1,\dots,n$.
  • Sender $i$ has a type $\theta_i\in\Theta_i$; common prior $\mu\in\Delta(\Theta)$. The receiver has no private information.
  • The receiver chooses $a\in A$. All sets are finite.
Payoffs and timing
Payoffs: $u_i(a,\theta)$ for $i=0,\dots,n$.
Timing: types realize $\to$ communication $\to$ receiver acts.

Application: $\Theta_i=\{L,R,Q\}$, $A=\{L,R\}\times\{1,2\}$.

Protocols

Protocol
A tuple $\big(\{M_i\}_{i\ge 1},\,M_0,\,\tau\big)$.
  1. Senders simultaneously report $m_i\in M_i$ to a black box.
  2. The receiver sees $m_0$, drawn according to $\tau:\prod_i M_i\to\Delta(M_0)$.
  3. The receiver acts.
Includes
Direct mechanisms: $M_i=\Theta_i$, $M_0=A$.
Transparent protocols: $M_0=\prod_i M_i$, $\tau$ the identity.

Equilibrium and the Revelation Principle

Strategies:

$$\sigma_i:\Theta_i\to\Delta(M_i), \qquad \sigma_0:M_0\to\Delta(A).$$

An equilibrium is a BNE. It induces an outcome: a distribution on $\Theta\times A$.

Revelation principle (Myerson 1982, Forges 1986)
Absent off-protocol speech, every equilibrium outcome of every protocol is the truthful–obedient outcome of some incentive-compatible direct protocol.
  • Fix a protocol and an equilibrium with outcome $\eta\in\Delta(\Theta\times A)$ and conditionals $\hat{\tau}:\Theta\to\Delta(A)$. The direct mechanism $\hat{\tau}$ is truthful and obedient.
  • So if we only care about implementable outcomes, it is without loss to focus on truthful and obedient direct mechanisms.

Off-Protocol Communication

Fix a protocol and an equilibrium $(\sigma,\sigma_0)$; let $U_i^*(\theta_i)$ be sender $i$'s interim payoff. After the protocol delivers $m_0$, a sender may address the receiver privately.

Simple speech by sender $i$: $(S,\hat{\tau})$
$S\subseteq\Theta_i$: the claimed types.
$\hat{\tau}:M_0\to\Delta(A)$: the proposed adjustment.
Behavior inside the protocol is presumed on path.
Confession by sender $i$: $(S,\hat{\sigma},\hat{\tau})$
Adds $\hat{\sigma}:S\to\Delta(M_i)$, the claimed reporting strategy.
"I am in $S$, I reported according to $\hat{\sigma}$, now do $\hat{\tau}$."
The speeches we have seen

Credible Speeches

A simple speech $(S,\hat{\tau})$ is credible if
  1. given equilibrium play, exactly the claimed types strictly gain from the adjustment;
  2. at every consistent $m_0$, $\hat{\tau}(m_0)$ is optimal for the receiver taking the claim at face value;
  3. at every inconsistent $m_0$, the speech is ignored: $\hat{\tau}(m_0)=\sigma_0(m_0)$.
A confession $(S,\hat{\sigma},\hat{\tau})$ is credible if, on top,
the claimed report $\hat{\sigma}$ is a best response to the adjustment.
Robustness
An equilibrium is confession-proof if it admits no credible confession (and simple-speech-proof if no credible simple speech).

Results

Revelation Principle, Sender Side

Sender-direct
A protocol is sender-direct if $M_i=\Theta_i$ for every sender $i$.
Let $(\sigma,\sigma_0)$ be an equilibrium of a protocol in which every sender's strategy is pure and onto, and let $\tau':=\tau\circ\sigma$ be the sender-direct protocol with the same $M_0$. Then truthful reporting with $\sigma_0$ is an equilibrium of $\tau'$ inducing the same outcome, and it is confession-proof iff $(\sigma,\sigma_0)$ is.
  • Pure: each type sends one message. Onto: every message is sent by some type. Pooling is allowed.
  • Truthful reporting in any direct mechanism qualifies. Next: the hypothesis has bite.

More messages expand deviations and confessions, but also mimicking opportunities: credibility can be destroyed.

Present in three stages: reveal the speech and the x payoffs, then the extra message y, then the conclusion.

The canonical sender-direct construction translates every sender 1 report into x. The direct protocol therefore offers only x's distribution. Baseline sender payoffs are zero. Under the speech, a gains 5/8 and b loses 1/8. The receiver strictly prefers h at r and e at s conditional on a, and every report is optimal in the direct protocol.

Message y leaves Pr(r given L)=3/4 unchanged and lowers Pr(r given R) from 1/2 to 1/4. Type b likes h in L but dislikes it in R. His payoff becomes (3/4 - 2 times 1/4)/2 = 1/8. Type a still prefers x, which gives 5/8 rather than 1/2. Self-selection anticipates b's profitable strategy change and rejects the speech.

All alternative claims fail. If a claims probability q of y, Pr(L given a,r)=3/(5-q), and Pr(L given a,s)=1/(3+q). Hence the receiver must always choose h at r and e at s, and b always profits by choosing y. A claim of b forces e everywhere and yields no gain. A claim including both a and b requires incompatible strict best responses at r. Sender 2 cannot strictly gain. Thus the original equilibrium is confession-proof.

Every receiver delivery has positive probability at every submitted-message profile. Reversion is vacuous. On path, Pr(r)=5/8 and Pr(s)=3/8. Receiver payoffs from h are 3/5 and 1/3 respectively, below its payoff 1 from e.

This refutes the canonical sender-direct construction, not sender-direct implementability: the sender-direct protocol that maps report a to x's row and report b to y's row is pure and onto, induces the same outcome, and is confession-proof (it is the original protocol relabeled). Payoffs depend on the full type profile, as allowed in TM-02.

Three reveals: first the claim and its response with no information, then the row with disclosure, then the credibility comparison and intuition.

Both protocols are sender-direct. Both senders report truthfully, so sender strategies are pure and onto. Sender 1's report never affects deliveries, making every claimed report, including every mixed strategy, optimal. Sender 2 always gets zero. The example isolates receiver information from the previous slide's sender-message effect.

Equilibrium obedience is strict. Without information, receiver payoffs from $e,h,k$ are $2,5/4,3/4$. After observing $L$, they are $2,3/2,0$. After observing $R$, they are $2,1,3/2$. Thus the receiver chooses $e$ in either protocol, and every sender type gets zero.

With coarse information, the confession is $S=\{a\}$ with adjustment $h$ at the constant delivery and any claimed sender 1 report. Conditional on $a$, receiver payoffs from $e,h,k$ are $2,5/2,3/2$, so $h$ is uniquely optimal. Type $a$ gets $1$ and type $b$ gets $-1$, making self-selection exact. This establishes a credible confession.

With disclosure, a claim of $a$ forces $h$ at $L$ and $k$ at $R$, each uniquely optimal. Type $a$ gets $1$ and outsider $b$ gets $(-1+3)/2=1$. Asking for $h$ at both deliveries would deter $b$, but the receiver rejects that instruction at $R$, where $k$ gives $3$ instead of $2$.

The informative equilibrium is confession-proof, not just immune to this particular speech. For any claim $S=\{a\}$, the same forced responses attract $b$. For $S=\{b\}$, receiver optimality forces $e$ at both deliveries, so nobody gains. For $S=\{a,b\}$, receiver optimality requires $h$ conditional on $a$ and $e$ conditional on $b$ at $L$. These strict requirements are incompatible. Sender 2 never strictly gains. This exhausts all type sets, mixed claims, and mixed adjustments under belief-free credibility.

All receiver deliveries occur on path. Both informative deliveries are consistent with every confession by sender 1, so reversion plays no role in the argument. Sender 1's payoff depends only on its own type and the action.

The canonical truthful-obedient DRP always recommends $e$ and therefore conceals sender 2's type. It admits the coarse protocol's confession. Because every type profile has positive probability, any truthful-obedient DRP implementing the constant-$e$ outcome must recommend $e$ at every report profile. Hence no truthful-obedient DRP implements this outcome confession-proofly.

The result shows that disclosure can improve robustness, not that more disclosure always does. Suggested verbal link: richer sender messages change an outsider's available deviations under a fixed adjustment; richer receiver information changes which adjustment the receiver can rationally follow.

Revelation Principle with One Sender

Let $n=1$ and credibility be belief-free. Fix an equilibrium of any protocol with outcome $\rho$. The canonical DRP is incentive compatible, its truthful–obedient equilibrium induces $\rho$, and it is confession-proof iff the original equilibrium is.

Proof idea: confession-proofness depends on the protocol only through the interim payoffs $U^*$.

  • Extract a lottery. A claimed message in a credible confession induces a lottery $\lambda$ over actions: profitable for the claimant, receiver-optimal against every claimed type.
  • Keep exactly its gainers. $G(\lambda):=\{\theta:\,u_1(\lambda,\theta)>U^*(\theta)\}$ is nonempty and contained in the claim, so $\lambda$ stays optimal against every type in $G(\lambda)$.
  • Transfer the lottery. In any protocol with the same $U^*$, a full-support claim by $G(\lambda)$ with the constant adjustment $\lambda$ is credible. The canonical DRP preserves $U^*$.

For fixed preferences, only interim payoffs determine confession-proofness.

Receiver ties are allowed; with unique receiver favorites the lottery is a pure action.

This strengthens TM-03's original theorem. All type, message, and action sets are finite, and the credibility definition is belief-free. Neither unique per-type receiver best replies nor pure or onto equilibrium sender strategies are required.

Let $B_S=\bigcap_{\theta\in S}\arg\max_a u_0(a,\theta)$. Given a credible confession $(S,\hat\sigma,\hat\tau)$, choose a claimant $\bar\theta$ and a message $\bar m$ in its claimed support. The induced lottery is $\lambda(a)=\sum_z\tau(z\mid\bar m)\hat\tau(a\mid z)$. All its deliveries are consistent, so belief-free optimality puts the support of $\lambda$ inside $B_S$.

Honest play and strict self-selection imply $u_1(\lambda,\bar\theta)>U^*(\bar\theta)$. Every original outsider could send the same message and receive the same lottery, so none gains. Thus $\varnothing\ne G(\lambda)\subseteq S$, and $\operatorname{supp}\lambda\subseteq B_S\subseteq B_{G(\lambda)}$. Shrinking the type set is the step that removes the need for uniqueness.

Conversely, if a lottery is receiver-optimal for every type in its nonempty set of strict gainers, ask each claimant to use the same full-support distribution on the protocol's sender messages. Every reachable delivery becomes consistent. Propose the same lottery at each such delivery and revert at unreachable ones. Every sender message now induces the same lottery, so honesty is automatic and self-selection is exact.

The characterization depends only on preferences and $U^*$. The canonical direct mechanism preserves the outcome and hence $U^*$. Truthfulness follows by replicating original mixed deviations; obedience follows because a recommended action is optimal at every original posterior pooled into that recommendation.

Under unique receiver favorites, the common best-response set of any feasible claim is a singleton, so the lottery is a pure action and the original pure-neologism equivalence follows. With ties, pure neologisms may fail to detect profitable lottery confessions.

Do not infer that the claimed strategy can be held at equilibrium play. Full-support scrambling may be necessary. The simple-speech equivalence on the following slide needs separate correction; it is not used by this proof.

One Sender: Confessions and Neologisms

Neologism (Farrell 1993, belief-free)
$(S,c)$ is credible if exactly the types in $S$ prefer $c$ to equilibrium, $\;u_1(c,\theta)>U^*(\theta)\iff\theta\in S$, and $c$ is receiver-optimal against every type in $S$.
With one sender, belief-free credibility, and unique per-type receiver best responses, $$\text{confession-proof}\;\iff\;\text{neologism-proof}.$$
  • Confession $\Rightarrow$ neologism. Every consistent delivery induces the same action $c$. Claimants gain; outsiders can imitate, so none can prefer $c$.
  • Neologism $\Rightarrow$ confession. A full-support claim implements $c$ at every reachable delivery.

Every credible confession has a credible type-only counterpart.

Theorem 3 of the talk; TM-04. Both directions are statements about existence of credible speeches, so the proof directions are "confession implies neologism" and "neologism implies confession"; their contrapositives give the two halves of the equivalence.

Confession implies neologism. Take a credible confession $(S,\hat\sigma,\hat\tau)$. With one sender, belief-free optimality has no belief content: at every consistent delivery the adjustment must lie in the common best-response set of $S$, which under uniqueness is a single action $c$. A claimant's claimed message reaches only consistent deliveries, so it yields $c$; honest play and strict self-selection give $u_1(c,\theta)>U^*(\theta)$ on $S$. An outsider can send that same message and also get $c$, so self-selection bounds $u_1(c,\theta')\le U^*(\theta')$ off $S$. Hence $(S,c)$ is a credible neologism.

Neologism implies confession. Let every type in $S$ claim the same full-support distribution over the protocol's messages, so every reachable delivery is consistent; adjust to $c$ there and revert elsewhere. Every message by every type then yields $c$, so honest play is automatic and self-selection is exactly the neologism's.

Implementation qualification. The theorem characterizes when a credible confession exists; it does not say the confession can hold the claimed strategy at equilibrium play. Full-support scrambling of the claimed strategy may be necessary. In particular, "confession-proof iff simple-speech-proof" is false: TM-04 §6 has an equilibrium that admits no credible simple speech (claimed strategy equal to equilibrium play) yet admits a credible confession. The old inference "a credible simple speech yields a credible neologism, hence simple-speech-proof implies neologism-proof" had its contrapositive reversed. A sufficient condition for simple speeches to suffice is coverage: each claimant's equilibrium messages reach every reachable delivery (TM-04 §7).

Extension without uniqueness. Drop unique best responses and replace the action $c$ by a lottery $\lambda$ over actions: a credible lottery neologism is $(G(\lambda),\lambda)$ with $G(\lambda)=\{\theta:u_1(\lambda,\theta)>U^*(\theta)\}$ nonempty and $\lambda$ supported on actions optimal against every type in $G(\lambda)$. Then, with one sender and belief-free credibility, confession-proof iff no credible lottery neologism (TM-03 §8, TM-04 §3). Extract $\lambda$ from a claimant's message, shrink the claim to its exact gainers, transfer by full-support scrambling. With receiver ties, pure neologisms can miss a profitable lottery confession (TM-04 §8).

With several senders the equivalence fails: receiver optimality conditions on the others' types through the claimed report and the delivery, which is exactly what the expert's sabotage-and-confess speech exploits in the application.

The Existence Issue

One sender, two equally likely types, three actions. Payoffs (sender, receiver):

$U$$C$$D$
$t_1$$(3,3)$$(2,2)$$(1,0)$
$t_2$$(1,0)$$(2,2)$$(0,3)$

Receiver's best responses: $U$ against $t_1$, $D$ against $t_2$, $C$ when $\Pr(t_1)\in[\tfrac13,\tfrac23]$.

Cheap talk (Farrell 1993)
Unique equilibrium outcome: pooling on $C$, both types get $2$. "I'm $t_1$" is credible — $t_1$ would get $3$, $t_2$ would get $1$, the receiver plays $U$. No neologism-proof equilibrium.

Does a Mediator Rescue Existence?

With a mediator the set of communication equilibria is much larger: e.g. $t_1\mapsto\tfrac12 U+\tfrac12 C$, $t_2\mapsto C$ is incentive compatible.

No. In every incentive-compatible direct mechanism:

  • $U^*(t_1)<3$: giving $t_1$ the action $U$ for sure makes $U$ obedient only if $t_2$ rarely gets it — and then $t_2$ claims to be $t_1$.
  • $U^*(t_2)\ge 1$: $t_2$ can always claim $t_1$, and obedience keeps $t_1$'s lottery valuable enough to him (an LP; the minimum is exactly $1$).
So "I'm $t_1$; play $U$" is a credible neologism against every IC direct mechanism
By Theorems 2–3, no equilibrium of any protocol is confession-proof.

Confession-proofness is a Farrell-type test and inherits Farrell's non-existence. The mediator does not help.

Conclusion

  • Incentive compatibility is not enough. A sender can change her behavior inside the protocol and explain it outside. Robustness requires ruling out credible confessions.
  • Expert advice: accuracy versus concealment. With weak career concerns, concealing expertise can require less accurate decisions. Strong career concerns can protect concealment by making the expert's speech attractive to the quack.
  • One sender: a neologism test. Under belief-free credibility, every credible confession has a neologism counterpart, and direct mechanisms suffice.
  • Multiple senders: protocol details matter. Richer message spaces and more receiver information can undermine credibility by making speeches attractive to outsiders.

Design the protocol with the speeches it makes credible in mind.
